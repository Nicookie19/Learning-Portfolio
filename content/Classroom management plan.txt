Classroom Management Plan 
Subject area: Computer Programming 
Year level: 1st year college, about 35 learners per class, ages 18 to 20, 60 minute 
sessions held in a classroom with a computer lab used once a week. 
Learner profile: 
•
Age and interests: Early adolescents who are active on social media, games, and 
short videos. They respond well to projects that connect to what they already use 
apps, games, websites. 
•
•
Learning abilities: A mixed-ability class. A few learners already code on their 
own, many are beginners, and some read and follow written instructions slowly. 
•
•
Classroom behavior: Energetic and talkative, easily pulled toward phones and 
unrelated browser tabs, and some are shy about asking for help in front of peers. 
•
•
Possible challenges: Uneven access to devices and internet at home, low 
confidence after early errors in coding, group work in which one member does 
everything, and difficulty restarting after a break. 
•
Guiding belief: Most misbehavior is a signal that a need is unmet like clarity, confidence, 
belonging, or attention. My plan therefore prevents problems first, teaches expectations 
explicitly, and responds to behavior by looking at its cause before choosing a 
consequence. 

1. Rules and Expectations 
I will co create these with my learners during the first week so they feel that they belong. 
I will keep the list short, phrase each as what to do, and tie each to a reason. 
Making the rules clear, fair, and teachable 
•
Teach them like content. On Day 1 I model and practice each rule with examples 
and non examples (for example, a role-play of asking for help properly versus 
calling out. I will revisit the rules after long breaks and before lab days. 
•
Fair for everyone. The rules apply equally to all learners and to me. I will use the 
same reminders and the same steps for every student, and I will adjust supports, 
not standards, for learners with special needs. 
•
Visible and simple. Rules are posted on the wall and on the first slide of every 
lesson, with icons for learners who read slowly. 
•
•
Positive reinforcement. I will give specific praise ("Thank you for helping Ana 
trace her loop without typing for her"), a weekly "Bug Buster" shout-out for helpful 
behavior, and team points that lead to a class reward such as choosing the 
warm-up game. 
•
Student voice. At mid-term I will run a quick check-in (what is working, what 
should change) so the rules stay relevant.

2. Time Management 
Daily routine for a 60 minute class 
Transitions 
•
Predictable cues. I will use the same signals every day (a chime, a raised hand, 
and a countdown timer on screen) so that learners know what to do without extra 
instruction. 
•
Teach and rehearse transitions. Early in the term I will practice moving from 
lecture to pairs and to lab, with a target of under 90 seconds, and let the class 
beat its own record. 
•
Prepared materials. Handouts, starter files, and links are posted in advance on 
our class page or already open on the lab computers, so no time is lost waiting 
for materials. 
•
Clear directions in three parts. I give directions in three steps (what, how long, 
what to do when finished), post them on screen, and ask a learner to repeat them 
back. Protecting time-on-task

3. Discipline Matrix 
My approach is preventive first, restorative second, and corrective last. For each likely 
concern I look for the root cause before choosing an action, and I record repeated 
patterns to see what needs to change in my teaching.

Escalation ladder 
1.
Prevent: clear expectations, engaging tasks, and strong relationships. 
2.
Redirect: non-verbal cue, proximity, or a quiet reminder. 
3.
Private talk: one-to-one conversation about the cause and agreement on next 
steps. 
4.
Logical consequence: a consequence connected to the behavior (for example, 
loss of device privileges for misuse, repairing harm done). 
5.
Partnership: call or meeting with the parent or guardian, with guidance counselor 
involvement and a written support plan. 
6.
Referral: school administration for serious or repeated concerns, following the 
school's student handbook and child protection policy. 
7.
Principles I will follow: no public shaming, no punishment that harms dignity, consistent 
and fair application, documentation of repeated incidents, and always a path back to a 
fresh start. A classroom runs well when learners know what is expected, feel that their 
time is used well, and trust that I will respond to them fairly. By teaching expectations 
explicitly, designing routines that keep learners busy with meaningful tasks, and using a 
discipline approach that looks for causes first, I aim to create a classroom where every 
Grade 9 learner can focus, take risks with coding, and grow in responsibility.
